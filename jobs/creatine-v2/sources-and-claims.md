# Sources and claim map · Creatine v2

## Hair and baldness

**2009 rugby trial, PMID 19741313:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19741313/

Twenty college-aged rugby players volunteered; 16 completed the three-week trial. It measured serum testosterone and DHT after a 25 g/day loading week followed by 5 g/day for two weeks. It did not measure hair loss.

**2021 review, PMID 33557850:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33557850/

The abstract says there were over 500 peer-reviewed publications involving creatine; it does not establish a number-one ranking across all supplements in human history. It discusses the 2009 DHT result and the often-cited 12 subsequent studies examining testosterone, not 12 direct hair-loss replications. The review authors reported no human reports of hair loss and said available evidence did not indicate creatine causes baldness. The edit keeps the creator's natural superlative line with this visible qualification and restores the meta-study aside with a clear limit.

**2025 randomized trial, PMID 40265319:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40265319/

45 trained men were recruited; 38 completed 12 weeks at 5 g/day or placebo. No significant group-time differences were found for DHT or assessed hair outcomes. This is one short trial in healthy young men, not lifetime evidence or evidence for every population. Several authors disclosed sports-nutrition/supplement relationships.

## Kidney function

**2013 randomized trial, PMID 23680457:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23680457/

26 healthy, resistance-trained men eating at least 1.2 g protein/kg/day were followed for 12 weeks (12 creatine; 14 placebo). The study measured GFR using 51Cr-EDTA and found no significant group-time change. The “long-term” article title is more sweeping than the study's 12-week follow-up; this result does not establish safety for people with kidney disease.

Serum creatinine is a kidney-test marker affected by creatine metabolism; a changed marker alone does not establish injury. **2026 kidney meta-analysis, PMID 42507286:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42507286/ reports higher serum creatinine and lower creatinine-derived GFR estimates, but no significant difference in directly measured Cr-EDTA GFR. The authors interpret the discrepancy as likely metabolic rather than injury. The recorded absolute “completely normal and healthy” guarantee is excluded.

## Weight and body fat

**2003 controlled trial, PMID 14636103:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14636103/

17 active men were studied for four weeks using 30 g/day for two weeks, then 15 g/day for two weeks. Total body weight and measured body water rose in the creatine group, with no significant change in percent body fat in this small study. It does not show that fat gain is physically impossible and did not measure intracellular water distribution. The recorded “zero calories means impossible to gain fat” claim is excluded.

## Dose and label

The **2021 review, PMID 33557850** describes 3–5 g/day (or 0.1 g/kg body weight) as an effective smaller daily amount. The recording's 5 g statement is retained with the study-review context visible.

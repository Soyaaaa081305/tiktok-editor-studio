# Sources and claim map

## Hair / baldness

**[2009 rugby trial, PMID 19741313](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19741313/).** Twenty college-aged rugby players volunteered; 16 completed the three-week trial. It measured serum testosterone and DHT after a 25 g/day loading week followed by 5 g/day for two weeks. It did not measure hair loss. The edit says exactly that.

**[2021 review, PMID 33557850](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33557850/).** Discusses the 2009 DHT result and explains that the often-cited 12 subsequent studies investigated testosterone; those are not 12 direct replications of a hair-loss outcome. Review authors report that there were no human reports of hair loss and that the evidence did not indicate creatine causes baldness. The edit removes the recording's incorrect “12 studies replicated hair results” line.

**[2025 randomized trial, PMID 40265319](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40265319/).** Forty-five trained men were recruited; 38 completed 12 weeks at 5 g/day or placebo. No significant group-time differences were found for DHT or the assessed hair outcomes. This is one short trial in healthy young men, not evidence for every population or lifetime use. Several authors disclose sports-nutrition/supplement relationships; the card reports sample and duration and avoids a universal conclusion.

## Kidney function

**[2013 randomized trial, PMID 23680457](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23680457/).** Twenty-six healthy, resistance-trained men eating at least 1.2 g protein/kg/day were followed for 12 weeks (12 creatine; 14 placebo). The study measured GFR using 51Cr-EDTA and found no significant group-time change. The title's “long-term” is more sweeping than its 12-week follow-up. The video does not apply this result to existing kidney disease.

Serum creatinine is a kidney-test marker affected by creatine metabolism; a changed marker alone does not establish injury. The [2026 kidney meta-analysis (PMID 42507286)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42507286/) reports higher serum creatinine and creatinine-estimated GFR but no significant difference using direct Cr-EDTA GFR; it interprets the discrepancy as likely metabolic rather than injury. Its abstract states a mean creatinine increase of 0.14 mg/dL. The edit therefore removes the creator's “completely normal and healthy” guarantee and tells viewers that the small trial is not a kidney-disease safety result.

## Weight and body fat

**[2003 controlled trial, PMID 14636103](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14636103/).** Seventeen active men were studied for four weeks using 30 g/day for two weeks, then 15 g/day for two weeks. Total body weight and measured body water rose in the creatine group, with no significant change in percent body fat in this small study. It does not show fat gain is physically impossible. The edit removes that incorrect absolute claim and presents the trial's sample, duration, and unusual high-dose regimen.

## Dose and label

The [2021 review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33557850/) describes 3–5 g/day (or 0.1 g/kg body weight) as a smaller daily amount that can be effective. The recording's 5 g statement is retained as his own general recommendation, with the study-review context visible. The package label itself reads 5 g per serving, 30 servings, 150 g and “No approved therapeutic claims.” These are label facts, not efficacy verification. The creator's recommendation of the brand is clearly labeled as his personal pick.
